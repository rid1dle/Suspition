# Product

## Register

product

## Users

Friends playing together in one room, usually a living room at night: on sofas in dim light, the table screen on a TV or laptop across the room, a phone in every hand. Sometimes one or two people play alone together with the GM. They are not gamers looking for a system to master; they want a night of talking, accusing, bluffing and working it out. They glance at their phones for a few seconds at a time between talking out loud, so every screen is read in a glance, often while someone else is speaking.

The job: play a character in a living mystery all at once, together, and leave the night saying "we figured out what really happened", or "I fooled all of you".

## Product Purpose

Suspition is a social deduction role-play game for people in one room. Claude is the game master: it writes a fresh world for each group, holds the truth, plays the other characters, answers each player privately and keeps the story whole while the players drive it. Each player has their own phone; a shared table screen shows the public picture.

It is play-all-at-once, not turn-based: everyone talks, acts and investigates at the same time. Turn-based moments (a hot seat, a vote) are short beats inside that, never the strategy. The mystery works like Outer Wilds: the truth is fixed, and the room pieces it together from what it finds and says.

Success: the talk in the room is the fun, the screens never get in its way, and every story feels like its own (sorrowful, tense, funny, eerie) because the GM shapes it and the screens follow.

## Brand Personality

Tense, warm, alive, and changing with the story. The baseline is suspicion in the air among friends who are laughing, with a world that reacts all the time. But the feel is not fixed: when the GM writes a sorrowful story it should feel sorrowful; an eerie one should feel eerie. The screens carry the mood the story is in, at the edges.

Voice: plain, simple English. Short sentences, common words, no walls of text. Written for a 12-year-old, never childish.

References: Among Us and Werewolf apps (simple, social, quick to read), Secret Hitler and other social role-play games, story games like Lord of the Mysteries and Disco Elysium (atmosphere, a world that pulls you in), Outer Wilds (the joy of working out a fixed truth).

## Anti-references

- A SaaS dashboard: cards of settings, stats and pills everywhere; a tool, not a game.
- A wall of text: long paragraphs on a phone during a loud room.
- A turn-based board app: waiting for your turn, greyed-out screens, nothing to do.
- Cheap horror or neon gamer: blood-splatter fonts, RGB glow, skulls, mystery-game clichés.

## Design Principles

1. **The talk in the room is the game.** Screens support it and get out of its way: glanceable in two seconds, then back to the people.
2. **Show only what can change this game.** A choice that cannot change anything for this group is hidden, not disabled. Rare choices fold away. Fewest options always.
3. **Readability first, mood second.** The world's look is welcome at the edges and in titles; reading always wins. Text sits on solid surfaces in one clean font.
4. **The story sets the mood.** The GM shapes the story; the screens follow its mood (tint, pace, a short glow on a turn), subtle but noticeable, never in the reading area.
5. **Mixed register by screen.** Phones serve the play (product: familiar, quick, private). The table screen is the shared show everyone watches (closer to brand: big, atmospheric, readable from the sofa).

## Accessibility & Inclusion

Standard good practice: readable contrast in a dim room, table-screen text big enough to read from across the room, respect reduce-motion. A culprit's phone must look exactly like an innocent's: no screen may reveal a side by its layout.
